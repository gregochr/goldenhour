Which ground the field should sit on

Same field, same locations, same window, six basemaps. All six maps are synced — drag or zoom any one and the rest follow — because the answer changes with scale: a basemap that reads at the regional glance can be useless at the headland you are driving to. The thing to judge is not which map is prettiest on its own but which one disappears: the field is the data, and the ground under it is there to say where and nothing else.

The recommendationStay dark, but change two things. Drop the basemap's own place labels — GLASGOW, Morpeth, Ashington are competing with our location chips for the same pixels, and ours carry a rating the basemap's cannot. And warm the tiles into the palette: the stock dark tiles are neutral blue-grey inside a warm brown UI, so the map currently reads as a third-party component embedded in the app rather than part of it. A CSS filter does it at zero tile cost.
Why not lightThe field is a translucent warm ramp — red through amber to green. On a light ground the low end of that ramp is the least visible part, which inverts the meaning: poor should be quiet, not invisible, and go should be the thing your eye lands on. A dark ground also lets the field carry the glow that makes a warm blob read as light rather than as a shape. Positron is in the grid so you can see it rather than take my word.
The case for terrainThis is the one genuinely open question. A hillshaded dark basemap shows aspect, valleys and the shape of the coast — which is what a landscape photographer is actually reading a map for: does that edge face west, will fog pool in that dale, is there a headland to shoot from. It costs contrast against the field, so it is a real trade rather than a free win.
The provider decision, forcedThe shipped app's attribution says Tiles © Esri while this prototype was on CARTO — and CARTO's no-label styles now return tiles with API KEY REQUIRED drawn into the image, so the one change with the clearest benefit is not available on the current provider without a key. Esri serves the whole set unwatermarked, including the layer the app already credits. That effectively settles it: go where the app already is, and choose deliberately rather than inheriting whichever prototype the code came from.
