Coming up starts where the forecast stops

Plan owns the next four days and there is always something in them — a hot topic on every window, an inversion most mornings, dust in the air since June. So Coming up cannot be a feed of what is happening. Two things earn a row: a date fixed by orbital mechanics, and the forecast peak of a standing condition. Everything else about those conditions stays in the strip at the top, one click from view — held back, never discarded.

Surprise — rarity and magnitude, and the trade between them

Infrequent and large needs attention. Infrequent and average still gets it. Frequent and small is still good information — you just might choose to ignore it. So the bands are not a filter that throws things away; they are an escalation of who starts the conversation. Everything stays reachable at every level. The two measures compensate rather than gate, and both are surprisal in bits, so they add: S = rarity + magnitude. Rarity is log₂ of the mean gap in days — daily ≈ 0, weekly ≈ 2.8, monthly ≈ 4.9, annual ≈ 8.5. Magnitude is −log₂ P(X ≥ x) against the topic's own history — median ≈ 1, p90 ≈ 3.3, p99 ≈ 6.6.

“Standing condition” stops being a category you assign and becomes one you observe: a topic whose typical occurrence sits in the bottom-left. Its strip line is not a dismissal — it is the handle on everything being held back, and it opens to show every occurrence with the score it got. If a routine 4/10 plume is what you wanted today, it is one click away.

nothing is
discarded
The three bands are on request, in the list and announced — delivery, not worth. A frequent, average occurrence is real information and stays one click inside its condition line, with the score it scored. The failure mode to avoid is not showing too little; it is silently showing too little.
band edges
are knobs
5, 7.5 and 9.5 are placeholders. Set them against a year of real backlog with a target rate — roughly 10 badges a year, of which one or two clear the top contour, and a chronology that stays under about fifteen rows. The shape of the surface is the design decision; the exact contours are calibration.
two badges,
one scale
The count badge and the solid ◆ are the third and fourth bands of the same surface, not two kinds of judgement. Above 9.5 there is only ever one thing, so the badge drops the number. The Orionids clear it on rarity alone — annual, at 8.5 — while the king tide reaches 9.0 on magnitude and stays a count. Neither is “more important” than the other in any sense the model can express beyond the total.
rarity is not
unpredictability
A spring tide is perfectly predictable and still only fortnightly. Measuring the arrival's information would score it zero and drop it off the surface entirely; measuring its rate puts it at 3.9, which is why a median run sits just inside on request and a king tide clears the top band. Predictability governs confidence, not importance.
never add
linked causes
The king tide and the supermoon are one piece of orbital mechanics. Summing their surprisal double-counts a single cause into a false alarm — take the maximum. Only genuinely independent coincidences add, and those are the ones worth a badge.
evidentiary bar
and hysteresis
≥ 5 occurrences in the trailing 60 days before a rate is trusted — two storms in sixty days gives a 95% Poisson interval of roughly 0.4–11, which is no answer. Rates are re-estimated nightly on a trailing 60 days, with a 1-bit dead zone at each band edge so a topic sitting on a contour does not move every night.
seasonality
moves things
up the axis
Dust at one a week scores 2.8 in August. By November the gap stretches to a month and it scores 4.9 — the same plume, two bands higher, and the first one after a quiet autumn earns a row on rarity alone. Nothing special-cased; the axis just moved.
cold start
Under 60 days of history, no rate and no percentile is trustworthy. Assume high rarity, list it, do not badge. Over-listing something new for two months is a smaller failure than silently swallowing it.

What a condition then contributes

intensity
distribution
The topic's own score history over a trailing 90 days, as a median and a p90. Each condition keeps its own scale — inversion strength 0–10, dust load 0–10, tidal range in metres — so nothing is ever compared across types, only against its own past.
peak
Highest forecast or predicted value in the forward window, gated on landing within 90 minutes of a light window. A 9/10 inversion at two in the afternoon is not a peak worth listing, whatever its surprisal. This is the single dated thing a standing condition contributes.
eligibility
Only dates beyond Plan's four days can badge. Inside that horizon there is always something, so nothing there can ever fire it.

What changed

Conditions are not eventsDust arrives about once a week and has done since June; there is an inversion most mornings. Neither is news by now, and neither has a single date to sort by — but both have peaks, and peaks are dated. Standing conditions get one line each saying how often they run; what they contribute to the chronology is the day they are at their best.
Tides work the same wayA spring tide every fortnight is a standing rhythm, not seven pieces of news. It gets one condition line — 7 runs in 90 days, biggest 12 Sept — and the runs themselves stay in the chronology as single rows, because unlike dust each one is a discrete dated window you might drive to.
The two tabs stop overlappingEvery one of the next four days carries a topic, so listing them here would just be Plan again in a worse format. The handoff row states the boundary out loud — now to Mon 31, three topics live, on Plan — and the chronology begins after it. Plan answers is tonight worth it; Coming up answers when is the next one. Neither answers the other.
Say the definition onceSeven of eleven rows were tide runs, six carrying the identical sentence about lunar alignment and an identical "Figures for St. Mary's Lighthouse" footer. The next run explains itself in full; the rest are one line each. That alone removes about a third of the page.
The wave does the comparingThe drilldown's tide sparkline, reused — but the amplitude is now the range, so the 5.2 m king tide stands visibly taller than the 5.0 m run three weeks after the equinox. A faint average tide sits behind each wave, which is the +1.9 vs avg figure drawn rather than said. The marker holds high or low water on the window.
Coincidence is one eventThe king tide run and the November supermoon were two unrelated rows. They are the same piece of orbital mechanics, and their overlap is the best coastal date of the quarter — so they are one card with two swatches, and the card says why they coincide.
Colour is the existing topic setTeal coastal, green aurora, steel air, purple night sky, gold sun and moon — the same five hues already carried by the heat pills, the tide row and the drilldown. No new vocabulary, and the tab stops reading as monochrome.
The badge is silent by defaultNothing inside Plan's four days can ever fire it — there is always something there. It counts only arrivals that are rare for their own type: a spring tide run entering at day 90 is silence, because there is one every fortnight forever; a king tide is one a year. A handful of times a year is the only rate at which a badge stays worth looking at.
Forecast never badgesAurora, inversion, dust and snow are live several times a week and already have the banner and the pills on Plan. The Coming up badge belongs to the almanac side — the fixed dates that have no other alarm.
Rarity escalates the shape, not the numberThe count badge and the solid ◆ are the top two bands of one surface. Above 9.5 bits there is only ever one thing in play, so the badge stops counting. The Orionids get there on rarity alone; the king tide gets to 9.0 on magnitude and keeps a number.
The badge has to land somewhereA count with nothing to find is a dead end, so the same state marks the entries: a line at the top saying what arrived, and a NEW flag on the row. One click marks it seen and the badge clears.
