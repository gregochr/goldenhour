Heat that reads as heat

The field currently runs red at 1★ and green at 5★ — the verdict palette, borrowed because it was already there. On a map, that inverts the one convention everybody arrives with: warmer means more. This changes the default field to a temperature scale, cold slate at 1★ through to red at 5★, and keeps the verdict ramp as a preference for anyone who learned the old map. Markers, the Plan thumbnails and both score bars move onto the same ramp, so the whole surface speaks one colour language.

The verdict ramp above is the one in heatField.js, and it is not the --color-verdict-* tokens. frontend/src/index.css ships go #16a34a, marginal #d97706, standdown #b91c1c, awaiting #6b7280 — saturated web colours, not these muted ones. HeatmapGrid.jsx consumes those tokens live.

The same goes for this document's own chrome. It is drawn in the proposed Kodachrome skin, but index.css still ships the blue-grey plex palette — --color-plex-surface is #252830, not the brown the handoff notes claim. Treat every warm hex here as a proposal, and every hex attributed to a file as shipped. Reconciling the two palettes is a separate job, and this change does not depend on it.

The low end is a cool slate blue rather than grey, so a poor night still reads as a deliberate colour instead of missing data — and it sits in the palette already as a desaturated cousin of --color-tide. The cool half runs all the way to 2.8, the crossover sits at 3, and red arrives by 4.3. The five tokens are sampled from this ramp at whole stars; §05 lists them.

The stops are deliberately uneven. Regional means in a real week occupy about 1.9–4.6, and two thirds of location scores fall between 2.5 and 4.5 — so an evenly spaced ramp spends its blue and its red on values that never survive the blur, and paints every night the same orange. Spacing the stops onto the band the data actually occupies is what makes the scale discriminate. Interpolation and the 1–5 clamp are unchanged.

Read down the right-hand column: Thursday sunrise and Friday sunrise are the two nights to skip, and they are the two that stay cold. That is the whole job. Note this is absolute, not normalised against the week — a 3.0 looks like a 3.0 in every week, or poor → worth it stops being a promise.

Where the fractional stops actually appear. A location's rating is an integer 1–5 — so no pin will ever be coloured by the 2.2 or 3.9 stop. Every fractional value in the field comes from spatial blending between integer-scored neighbours, and every value in the table above is a regional mean. That is precisely why the ramp needs stops between the stars: the inputs are coarse, the field is not.

04

The week on the temperature scale

The strip is the test that matters: at thumbnail size the field has to be legible as a shape before a word is read. Cold-to-hot gives it a single axis to be read along, and with the stops re-spaced the two bad mornings drop out of the week visibly — Thursday and Friday sunrise go cool while the three good evenings hold their heat. The old ramp asked the eye to tell red from green at 90px wide, which is also the most common form of colour blindness.

05

Markers, and the five tokens

Whole starswhat a marker shows
Interpolatedwhat a cluster shows

Markers are discrete, so they sample the ramp at whole stars — and those five samples are the tokens --color-heat-1 … --color-heat-5 that everything non-map uses. A 3★ pin lands on the crossover, which is correct: the map should not flatter it. Shown at the shipped geometry: buildMarkerSvg fills a circle at r=17 inside a 44×​44 box — 34px — with the arc track ringing it at r=19.

3★ gets its own ramp stop, because ratings are integers. With continuous scores the crossover could be left to interpolate; with five discrete values, 3★ is likely the most common rating in the catalogue, so whatever colour it lands on is the colour most of the map wears. Interpolating straight from 2.8 to 3.2 put it on a dun khaki — the muddiest point on the ramp — so there is now an explicit stop at 3.0 holding it to a clean gold. This costs the field nothing: blur covers the slightly sharper transition, and "middling" reading as the start of warm rather than as mud is the honest result.

Label-bearing fills snap to whole stars — the rule, and the reconciled numbers

readableInkOn picks the better of two inks, which only helps where one of them clears 4.5:1. Every ramp through mid-luminance has runs where neither does, because the two inks fail from opposite directions. Scanned at 0.01★ against the pair the app actually ships — #0F172A and #FFFFFF — and reported as the share of the 401 samples that fail, not as summed run width; the two conventions differ by one step per run:

13.2% of samples, in three runs — 2.48–2.60★, 4.10–4.24★, 4.37–4.61★. Two of the three sit in the hot half, and that is the finding worth carrying: the failures cluster where 4★ and 5★ ratings live, not at the cold end where the eye expects trouble. The 4.3 stop #D63A26 is a dark saturated red — the worst case for both inks at once, with dark ink at 3.82:1 and white reaching only 4.67:1 — so the 3.9 → 4.3 → 5 leg crosses the dead zone twice. A 4.15★ cluster gets 4.26:1 at best, on a fill that looks bold and confident.

The third row is why two independent scans disagreed. This document originally scanned with its own bone #F2E7D3 rather than the app's white, which reports 21.7% across two runs — worse, because bone gives up contrast on the dark stops that white holds. Bone was never shipping; the app's white ink is the better of the two and needs no change.

The rule: any fill that carries a label samples the ramp at whole stars. Only label-free surfaces interpolate. All five whole stars clear 4.5:1 under the shipped pair, so markers are already safe. Cluster badges are not — starsFromAverage returns avg / 20, continuous, so a cluster paints an interpolated fill and puts a count on it. Snapping the cluster fill is the whole fix, and it returns the fill to the resolution the data has: rating is an integer.

Note what snapping does and does not buy. The interior of the ramp is not safe — it is merely no longer sampled by anything that carries text. The heat field keeps interpolating, correctly, because nothing in it is labelled. If a labelled surface ever goes continuous, the 4.3 stop is the one to fix first. The second-order gain is the test: it.each([1, 2, 3, 4, 5]) passes today by luck — it tests the only five values never at risk — and snapping makes it correct by construction. Add one test asserting the invariant directly.

markerUtils.js RATING_COLOURS — grey at 1★, then dark olive gold climbing to full gold — was already deleted in D3. Its stepped 0–100 twin scoreColour() is the one still live, driving cluster badges and any marker with scores but no rating. That is the remaining duplicate to retire.

06

Score bars

The bars exist twice. PopupScoreRow inside components/MarkerPopupContent.jsx steps the score into four buckets on the gold ramp — so 26 and 49 are the same colour — while PlanScoreBar on the Plan side fills with gradients instead. The same score therefore renders differently depending on which surface you are looking at. Only the popup ladder below is read from source; PlanScoreBar's gradient stops were not, and ScoreBar.jsx, which this section previously cited, was deleted in D4.

That gold ramp is the marker ramp. Counting scoreColour(), the app still implements one colour language three times and gets a different answer from each. The work here is not building bars — it is deleting two of the three and pointing the survivor at the ramp.

Continuous, not bucketed, and solid rather than gradient. A bar has one value, so it gets one colour, sampled off the ramp at that value exactly as a marker is — which also makes 26 and 49 finally distinguishable. The earlier Popup Tidy.html spec called for a gradient fill; that worked when the ramp was gold→red but turns into a five-hue rainbow now that it starts cold.

How a 0–100 metric joins a 1–5 scale — the part that needs measuring

The ramp is indexed 1–5 because ratings are. Fiery Sky and Golden Hour are 0–100, so they need a mapping, and the obvious one is wrong. Assuming 0–100 spans the full ramp asserts that these metrics use their whole range — and they do not. Every fixture in the test suite sits between 50 and 78, which the naive mapping squeezes into 3.0–4.1: gold to orange, never cold, never hot. The bars would technically be on the temperature scale while showing almost none of it.

So the ramp is shared but the calibration is per metric: HeatField.rampPct(value, lo, hi), where lo and hi are that metric's real working range. The fourth row below uses 25–85 as an illustration. Those two numbers must come from the score distribution, not from a designer — run a percentile over a season of stored forecasts and set them to roughly the 5th and 95th. Until that is measured, treat the calibrated row as the shape of the answer rather than the answer.

One thing to watch

With both bars on the same ramp, Fiery Sky 60 and Golden Hour 60 are the same colour — the bars stop being distinguishable at a glance and the labels do all the work. That is the price of one scale, and it is worth naming before it ships.

If it reads badly in the popup, the cheap fix is not a second ramp: it is giving Golden Hour a thinner track (4px against 6px) so the pair stays readable by weight rather than by hue.

The tint is interpolated, never hard-coded: a Fiery Sky of 18 lands in the cold end and reads muted, an 87 lands in the red. Both implementations carry tests — test/ScoreBar.test.jsx and test/MarkerPopupContent.test.jsx — which need updating whichever way this goes.

07

What changes

  1. heatField.js — STOPS becomes STOPS_TEMP and STOPS_VERDICT with a module-level MODE, read by ramp(). Everything downstream of ramp() is unchanged. The patched kernel in this folder is the reference.
  2. Keep the uneven stop spacing. It is the difference between a scale that discriminates and one that renders every night orange. The 2.2 stop's darkness is also load-bearing — it is what lets the marker label clear contrast. If the score distribution shifts once real forecasts replace the mock, re-measure and re-space — do not fall back to even stops.
  3. New tokens — --color-heat-1 … --color-heat-5 in index.css, sampled from the ramp at whole stars. The --color-verdict-* tokens are untouched and unrelated: they are saturated web colours used for verdict words, not the muted ramp in heatField.js.
  4. One-time notice on the map after update, dismissible, stating that colours now run cold to hot. Not a setting — a sentence.
  5. Settings — a new Map Colours section in components/UserSettingsModal.jsx: mapColourScale: 'temp' | 'verdict' defaulting to temp, plus markersFollowScale defaulting on. Persist through settingsApi like the rest of the modal, and leave it outside the isPro gate.
  6. markerUtils.js — scoreColour(), the stepped 0–100 twin, retired in favour of HeatField.ramp() and gated on markersFollowScale. RATING_COLOURS is already deleted (D3) — leave it alone; the MarkerIcon.test.jsx assertion against it went with it.
  7. Marker label ink — do not touch it. #627 already superseded the hard 3★ threshold this document originally specified: readableInkOn derives the ink per fill, which is better. Reintroducing a threshold would be a regression.
  8. Snap label-bearing fills to whole stars — the invariant from §05. Markers already comply; createClusterIcon does not, because starsFromAverage returns avg / 20. Snap the cluster fill, and add a test asserting no labelled fill is ever handed a fractional star.
  9. Marker arcs — buildMarkerSvg hard-codes #f97316 for the Fiery Sky arc and #E5A00D for Golden Hour. If both score bars are moving onto the heat ramp, these two arcs are the same two numbers in the same component and should move with them, or the popup and the pin will disagree.
  10. Calibrate the two 0–100 metrics before shipping the bars. HeatField.rampPct(v, lo, hi) exists for this; lo and hi come from a percentile over stored fierySkyPotential and goldenHourPotential values, one pair per metric. Shipping the naive 0–100 mapping puts every bar in the gold band and wastes the scale.
  11. Score bars — collapse two into one. The live duplication is PlanScoreBar (Plan side, gradient fills) against PopupScoreRow in components/MarkerPopupContent.jsx (map popup, four buckets). ScoreBar.jsx was deleted in D4 — ignore earlier references to it. Collapse the two into one component with a continuous solid fill sampled from HeatField.rampPct(); PlanScoreBar's gradients are the thing being removed. Update whichever tests cover them.
  12. Legend — the 60×6px ramp bar redraws from the active ramp. The words poor → worth it do not change.
