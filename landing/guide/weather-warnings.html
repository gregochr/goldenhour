Guide/Weather warnings

Weather warnings.

PhotoCast shows the Met Office's severe weather warnings next to the forecast, so you can see when a warning covers somewhere you were thinking of going. This page explains what the warnings are, where they appear and what they do not do.

What a warning is

A Met Office weather warning is the Met Office's own alert that severe weather may affect an area: rain, thunderstorms, wind, snow, lightning, ice, extreme heat or fog. PhotoCast does not write or judge warnings. It shows the ones the Met Office has issued, in the Met Office's words, wherever they cover at least one of the locations PhotoCast forecasts.

Each warning is shown with its level, its weather type or types, a headline, what to expect and the times it is valid. Everyone who can see the forecast sees the same warnings, on Lite and on Pro.

Warnings answer a different question from PhotoCast's ratings. A warning is about whether the weather could be disruptive or dangerous. A rating is about whether the light looks promising for a photograph. They are shown side by side and never combined.

Yellow, Amber and Red

The level is the Met Office's own. It is always written as a word as well as shown in a colour, so you never have to rely on colour alone. The Met Office's action words for the three levels are:

Yellow
Be aware.
Amber
Be prepared.
Red
Take action.

For what each level means for a particular warning, read the warning itself and the Met Office's own pages (see What to do).

A chip names the level and the weather type, for example "Amber warning of snow and wind". When several warnings cover the same place they are listed most severe first, then by impact, likelihood and start time.

Likelihood and impact

The Met Office sets a warning's level from two things: how likely the weather is, and how much impact it could have. The window popup on At a Glance draws the Met Office's matrix for each warning, with Likelihood up the side, Impact along the bottom and a tick in the cell for that warning. Each goes from 1 to 4, and the popup also gives the position as text for screen readers, for example "Likelihood 3 of 4, impact 2 of 4". PhotoCast adds no words of its own for the numbers.

The colour of the cell shows how the two combine. The level shown on the warning is always the one the Met Office issued.

Updated and raised

Updated
The Met Office has issued a newer version of this warning since it first appeared, for example to change the area, the times or the details. The window popup shows what changed in an "Update" line.
Raised from
On a Coming up entry, the warning's level has gone up in the last 24 hours, for example "Raised from Amber" on a Red warning. A raised warning is listed first on its day.

A warning that has been cancelled or has expired is removed.

Where warnings appear

Each of these shows the warning's level as a word and its weather type, and carries the Met Office attribution and a link to the Met Office warnings page.

At a Glance lanes and card marker
Warnings run as lanes above the sunrise and sunset cards, and a small marker on a card names the strongest warning for that window. More on At a Glance.
Window popup
Opening a sunrise or sunset shows each warning in full: the headline, what to expect, what to do, the dates, the matrix and how many places in each area are covered.
Location sheet and map card
A chip on each sunrise and sunset in a location's forecast, and on the map card for that location. More on the Map.
Coming up
Warnings that reach beyond the days on At a Glance have their own entries in the dated list. More on Coming up.
Map chip row
On the Map, chips for the sunrise or sunset you are looking at sit under the topic chips. The map itself shows no warning outlines or shading.
Ask PhotoCast
When your own question is about a place or day a warning covers, the answer may mention it in the Met Office's own words, with the warning, its dates and the credit beneath the answer. Ask never says there are no warnings, and its picks and ratings are exactly as served.

Warnings are tied to a sunrise or sunset. A night, high-water or past window on the Map shows none.

Warnings and star ratings

A warning never changes a star rating, a verdict, a best pick or the colours on the map. Ratings are worked out from the cloud, light, air and tide, and nothing from a warning goes into them. A warning is shown beside a rating, not inside it.

So a location can have a promising rating and a warning at the same moment. Treat the warning as the stronger message: a good sky is not worth a risky journey, and PhotoCast does not tell you whether somewhere is safe to go. In the same way, a poor rating does not mean the weather is dangerous.

How fresh they are

PhotoCast checks the Met Office feed every few minutes and refreshes what you see while the page is open. A new, changed or cancelled warning should appear or disappear within minutes of the Met Office showing it, and PhotoCast aims never to be more than about ten minutes behind.

If PhotoCast cannot reach the Met Office feed for around ten minutes, the warning areas show a line such as Warnings last checked 14:05 — check the Met Office. While that line is up, what you see may be out of date: a new warning may be missing, and one the Met Office has withdrawn may still be showing.

The absence of a warning here is never an all-clear. PhotoCast never says "no warnings". A place with nothing shown may still be affected, for example when the line above is up, or when a warning covers a place PhotoCast does not forecast. Always check the Met Office before you travel.

What to do

  • Read the warning in full on the window popup, and check the Met Office for the very latest: Met Office weather warnings.
  • For advice on what to do in severe weather, see the Met Office's What should I do page.
  • Think about the whole trip, not only the sunrise: the road, the walk, the coast and the tide.
  • Follow the advice of the emergency services, and tell someone where you are going.

PhotoCast is a planning tool, not a safety service. See the terms.
